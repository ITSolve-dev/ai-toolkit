# Synthesis

The planning literature was written for human teams that can talk while they work. The first thing this wiki has to establish, source by source, is which of its claims describe the work and which describe the people doing it.

After [[wake-invest]], the working position is that most of INVEST describes the work: overlap, value to a reader of the result, estimability, size and testability do not depend on who executes ([[invest]]). Negotiable is the exception, since it relies on a conversation during development.

After [[humanizing-work-splitting]], the cutting half of the wiki has a procedure, and the position holds. The splitting patterns are triggered by features of the work and judged by whoever reviews it, so changing the executor leaves them standing. The source also supplies the one move a size-driven view would miss: a task that fails on value is combined, not cut further ([[component-task-masquerading]]).

[[ambig-swe]] sharpens the position with the wiki's only measurement. A task the literature could leave thin, because someone would ask, cannot be left thin when the executor tends not to ask: in the benchmark the six models rarely asked unprompted, and having the information in the task beat obtaining it during the run for all six. So the thesis for task content is that the conversation moves into the task before the agent starts ([[conversation-moves-into-the-task]]). This holds on stripped issues with a simulated user, and whether it holds with real requesters is open.

[[radzikowska-waiting-blocked]] extends the split between work and people to the board. Its criteria for waiting and blocked describe the work and survive a change of executor ([[waiting-versus-blocked]]). Its mechanisms for limits, tension and stress, describe people and do not, although the limit still stops an agent mechanically ([[blocked-work-counts-against-wip]]). It also gives the charter's first working test something it lacked, an observable failure: a task that reaches a stopped state after starting ([[sequence-versus-wait]]).

[[kanban-guide]] gives the board half of the wiki a definition to stand on, and it is mostly about the work: states, counts, timestamps. It removes one assumption and adds one question. Its WIP is set by position and not by activity, so stopped work counts unless a project declares otherwise ([[wip-control]]). The question is what a bound protects when the executor can run many tasks at once. This wiki's working answer is the attention of the people steering, which no source yet tests.

[[scrum-guide-2020]] mostly fails the charter, as expected, since it describes people and meetings. What survives is narrow and changes what the plugin must report. The commitment is a goal and the items are a forecast ([[commitment-over-a-period]]), so a period judged by items finished is judged on the wrong thing. The two guides turn out not to disagree on limits. The Kanban Guide bounds the moment and the Scrum Guide bounds the period, and each allows the other's addition. An agent changes that from a matter of taste to a gap: an iteration setup relied on people's hours to restrain the moment, and an agent has no such restraint ([[board-rules-by-setup]]). What this wiki keeps from Scrum is not Scrum by the Guide's own definition, so no benefit Scrum claims can be inherited by assumption.

[[wester-board-design]] gives the board half its first test of drawing: a column is for what happens in a predictable place, after which work goes forward ([[column-versus-attribute]]). It disagrees with the Kanban Maturity Model on holding areas, and the disagreement is resolved on [[parking-lot]] by narrowing the parking lot to a declared exception. It also corrects this wiki. A planned review is a handoff and not a block, so the test of finishing without waiting applies to the executor's part ([[sequence-versus-wait]]). This matters because an agent-run task almost always ends in a review. Wester's post, the Kanban Guide and the Kanban Playbook come from one school, so their agreement is not independent evidence.

[[kanban-playbook-workflow-states]] settles the charter's open question about states, as far as these sources can. There is no canonical column set. There are criteria for judging columns and a set of distinctions any board must let a reader make, both on [[distinguishable-work-states]]. The Playbook treats the familiar five-column list as the failure case, and its remedy is to derive states from how work moved ([[mapping-workflow-states]]). The plugin therefore needs a classification to map columns onto and not a board to impose.

[[kanban-playbook-right-sizing]] gives a flow setup a size test: is there a good chance this finishes inside the SLE ([[size-ceiling]])? The page overstates it in two ways, since the question is itself an estimate, and since it reads an SLE it says is not reliable yet. What it contributes more durably is the point that the metrics count items, so the cut can flatter them both ways ([[hidden-wip-in-large-items]], [[component-task-masquerading]]). So the numbers alone cannot audit a cut, and the task text has to be read as well ([[compound-task-text-signs]]).

[[black-swan-qualitative-cod]] fills the empty centre of the ordering group, on a thin base. It is one unsigned practitioner article with no measurement, it calls itself a starting point, and its matrix image is lost, so only two of the nine combinations are the source's and the rest are this wiki's ([[ordering-by-value-and-urgency]]). What it contributes is a change of question: not what a task is worth, but what waiting loses ([[cost-of-delay]]). The ingredients are the requester's facts, which the repository cannot show, so the thesis that the conversation moves into the task extends to order. Before a task is ranked, someone has to say what it is worth and what waiting does to that, in a sentence that a later fact can contradict. Without that sentence, the rule fails the charter's test for a rule that chooses. Dividing by duration carries over less well, because it needs numbers the rating does not have and a constraint that an agent moves ([[cost-of-delay-over-duration]]).

[[zimmermann-good-bug-report]] is the base's second measurement, and it is about humans. It reinforces the split between the work and the people, and shows the split in a new place. Most of its ranking of report items describes experienced developers who know their system, and one item, expected behaviour, turns over when the reader is an agent ([[incoming-report-content]]). Its central result is that reporters know what is wanted and still do not supply it, which limits what any template can do. It also shows that a closed duplicate is often lost information ([[duplicate-reports]]), and it gives the board half a warning it lacked: a task's lifetime does not read the quality of its text ([[flow-metrics]]).

[[kubernetes-issue-triage]] removes the charter's prediction that intake triage would be self-authored, on a condition. It is a process written for one of the largest open-source projects, and most of it is people, permissions and tools. What survives is the shape: a marker for what nobody has read, a small set of outcomes, reproduction before acceptance, and triggers for what has stopped ([[triage-procedure]]). Its numbers do not survive, since 20 and 90 days encode a project of strangers ([[closing-what-no-longer-applies]]). So the thesis holds in a new place: the structure of a practice can transfer when its settings do not. It also gives order a second voice on one point, a top level that stops other work ([[interrupting-started-work]]), and a disagreement, one scale against two ratings, that resolves by whether a project ships in releases ([[urgent-versus-important]]).

[[montgomery-tracker-smells]] tests the charter's claim that a rule's sign lets the same page advise and audit, and it qualifies the claim. Practitioners shown 31 smells often answered "no" or "it depends". A sign shows that something may be wrong, and the project's workflow decides whether it is, so an audit reports a question ([[smell-is-not-a-finding]]). This is the first source to push against a design principle of this wiki and not only to fill a topic. It supports the rule on task content from another side: the one smell the discussion reports as unrefuted is a fact known and not in the task ([[conversation-moves-into-the-task]]), which is the thesis about conversation seen on a board. It also corrects a sign this wiki would have used, since changing acceptance criteria was refuted by all who answered ([[testable-acceptance]]). The limits are firm. There are no thresholds, it reports perceptions, two of 26 participants work at small companies, and no agent appears. Its strongest item, the ignored issue ([[closing-what-no-longer-applies]]), rests on mechanisms of which one part transfers.

[[cohn-agile-estimating-planning]] changed the shape of the base, since over half of its pages rest on that one book. It fills what the earlier sources left open. There is now a way to size work ([[size-versus-duration]], [[story-points]]), a way to give a date and to run it again ([[velocity-range-forecast]]), ways to protect that date ([[choosing-a-buffer]]), and a way to read a gap between plan and fact ([[release-progress-forces]], [[chosen-delay-versus-slip]]). The ordering group gains a second voice that does not repeat the first. Cohn weighs value, cost, new knowledge and risk, and leaves urgency out ([[prioritization-factors]]), so it complements the qualitative cost-of-delay source and gives no independent support for it.

The book also refines the thesis. The split between the work and the people runs through single rules as well as between them, so a rule can survive while the reason given for it does not. Counting a task only when finished survives, because only not started and done can be known ([[all-or-nothing-counting]]). Reporting for the project and not by person survives as the right call, but the reasons Cohn gives are about people ([[velocity]]). Group estimation loses one reason of two ([[who-estimates]]). The ideal day as a unit, evaluation pressure and the hours arithmetic do not carry over at all ([[ideal-days]], [[comparing-estimate-to-actual]], [[iteration-capacity]]). So a page from this source has to say which part of a rule is the work and which is the team, and copying the rule with its reason would be the mistake. The date arithmetic carries a further condition, this wiki's and not Cohn's: it divides by a rate, and it is only as good as that rate is stable, which nothing here has measured for agent-executed work ([[velocity]]).

[[vacanti-actionable-agile-metrics]] supplies the half of the board wiki that Cohn left to iterations: how to read history and give a date without a period or an estimate. Its measures need only the dates work crossed two stated points ([[flow-metrics]], [[started-and-finished-points]]), so they fit the charter's refusal of logged hours, and the plugin can compute them from board history without a person keeping anything ([[capturing-flow-data]]). It also fills the gap the synthesis used to name: a flow setup now has a way to give a date, as a range with a probability read from finished work ([[forecast-range-and-probability]], [[service-level-expectation]], [[throughput-monte-carlo]]). For a board that is about to start, the rule that lets the plugin report at all is that few items are enough to read a median ([[how-much-flow-data]]).

The thesis about the work and the people gets its strongest case here, and a new limit. Most of the book is arithmetic on counts and dates, so it survives an agent executing almost whole. What does not carry over is the assumption of where the constraint sits. Vacanti's advice to cut WIP rests on a process limited by concurrency, and with an agent that is true only when review is what work is waiting for ([[starting-more-work]]). So a rule can be sound arithmetic and still need a reading of the board before it applies. The same goes for the data: flow methods treat a process as one population, and a change of executor or model starts a new one ([[throughput-monte-carlo]]).

The book disagrees with the rest of the base on two points, and neither was left open. On dates, it says against Cohn to forecast from Cycle Time distributions and not from points, and Siemens reported no correlation between the two. This wiki resolves that by history: flow where a stable history exists, relative estimates and a range where it does not, and sizing for cuts in both ([[dropping-estimates-for-flow-forecasts]], [[forecast-range-and-probability]]). On order, against [[black-swan-qualitative-cod]], it weighs value only at replenishment and pulls committed work for predictability, which this wiki reads as a difference of moment and not a contradiction ([[value-decided-at-replenishment]]). Both resolutions are this wiki's. The Siemens case is the only comparison between the two approaches in this base, and it is one organization's report on humans.

[[flow-metrics-scrum-teams]] repeats most of [[vacanti-actionable-agile-metrics]] and was read in part, and since Vacanti is its first author, the two agreeing is one voice. It changes what the plugin can output in three places. A period gets a third way to fill it, a count read from a simulation of daily Throughput, which takes no estimate and needs only finish dates ([[throughput-monte-carlo]]). This wiki resolves it against Cohn and the Scrum Guide by setup ([[filling-a-period]]). An audit of open work gets its own measure and an order of questions ([[work-item-age]], [[percentile-intervention-triggers]]). And blocked work gets three policies a project states in advance, which the source asks and this wiki answers ([[blocked-work-counts-against-wip]]).

The thesis holds again in a sharper form. The book's largest claim, that estimation is not needed, does not survive its own next chapter, because the count covers right-sized items and that check is an estimate ([[dropping-estimates-for-flow-forecasts]]). Its claim that a stuck item is usually too big describes a team at work, and on an agent's board the same item is more likely to be waiting for a person. Throughput history on such a board counts accepted results, so this is the first forecast in the base whose input sits where this wiki expects the constraint. That is a fit with the reading below and not a test of it.

Across the board half one reading now recurs, and it is this wiki's and not any source's: with an agent executing, the scarce thing is the attention of the people steering. It underlies [[wip-control]], [[filling-a-period]], [[throughput-monte-carlo]], [[service-level-expectation]], [[active-versus-queued]], [[hidden-wip-in-large-items]], [[cost-of-delay-over-duration]], [[interrupting-started-work]], [[conservation-of-flow]], [[flow-efficiency]] and [[reading-a-cfd]]. It is the largest untested claim in this wiki, and most of the agent notes on those pages rest on it.

## Where the position is weak

- Most signs on the task-content pages are this wiki's and not the sources', because the sources describe good tasks and rarely describe how a bad one looks. The same is true of the board pages: the guides state requirements and rarely say how a violation looks.
- Only [[ambig-swe]] and [[zimmermann-good-bug-report]] measure anything, the first on agents and stripped issues, the second by surveying people about bug reports. [[montgomery-tracker-smells]] is empirical too, but it reports interviews. Everything else here is practice from human teams, [[cohn-agile-estimating-planning]], [[vacanti-actionable-agile-metrics]] and [[flow-metrics-scrum-teams]] included, kept because its mechanism can be checked and not because its effect was shown. Vacanti's numbers come from a constructed simulation and from the Siemens case ([[vacanti-actionable-agile-metrics]], its case section), which is confounded, inconsistent in places and written from inside. The two guides are definitions, and they offer no evidence that either works.
- Only one case compares a flow setup with an iteration setup, and it is about 50 teams on regulated software ([[vacanti-actionable-agile-metrics]]). The selection rule on [[board-rules-by-setup]] is this wiki's, and the third setup, combining them, is permitted by both guides and tested by neither here.
- The Kanban sources other than [[radzikowska-waiting-blocked]] come from one school, so the column criteria on [[distinguishable-work-states]] have one voice behind them. [[montgomery-tracker-smells]] adds a second, on three criteria only, as perceptions. [[vacanti-actionable-agile-metrics]] is not independent of it either: the Guide's copyright names Daniel S. Vacanti, Inc. ([[kanban-guide]] L56), so the book and the Guide agreeing on flow metrics or an SLE counts as one voice, and [[flow-metrics-scrum-teams]] shares its first author with both.
- The failure signs have no thresholds, and this wiki has not supplied any. So an audit from this base can say that something is there and cannot say that there is too much of it. No sign has been tried on a real board ([[smell-is-not-a-finding]]).
- The base has sixteen sources and leans on two authors. Of its 180 pages, 94 cite [[cohn-agile-estimating-planning]], over half, 49 cite [[vacanti-actionable-agile-metrics]], about one in four, and 16 cite [[flow-metrics-scrum-teams]], which is Vacanti again. Most of the Cohn pages cite nothing else, and most of `sizing` and much of `periods` has one voice. Each is a practitioner's account. Cohn's was read from a pre-publication manuscript, and its Chapter 23 is fiction. Where the same claim recurs across many pages, the recurrence is not corroboration.
- Urgency in ordering rests on one article with no measurement, and seven of the nine ranks on [[ordering-by-value-and-urgency]] are this wiki's derivation. Cohn adds four factors and leaves urgency out, so no second source tests it. Reinertsen's book, the obvious second voice, reached the base only as a third-party summary and was rejected.
- Nothing yet covers an agent's own estimate, or what replaces a team's experience in sizing ([[size-ceiling]]). The sizing group is now large and is still entirely about human teams, and no page shows that points, or any relative size, predict an agent's run.
- A date under a flow setup now has a method and thin ground for a few people, and the same goes for a count of what fits a period. The one figure for how much Throughput a simulation wants is a rule of thumb in days, and a quiet board can meet it with few finishes ([[how-much-flow-data]]). The sources' worked examples rest on hundreds of items, a few people steering an agent may never have that many from one process, and the tail is what a small sample cannot show ([[how-much-flow-data]], [[cycle-time-percentile-lines]]). No page says how long a board with an agent stays one population, so how much of its history still counts is open.
- Whether the flow rules hold for agent-run work is untested. Every sign and threshold on those pages comes from human teams, and no page shows what a stable process looks like when the executor can run many tasks at once.
