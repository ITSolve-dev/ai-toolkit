# Synthesis

The planning literature was written for human teams that can talk while they work. The first thing this wiki has to establish, source by source, is which of its claims describe the work and which describe the people doing it.

After [[wake-invest]], the working position is that most of INVEST describes the work: overlap, value to a reader of the result, estimability, size and testability do not depend on who executes ([[invest]]). Negotiable is the exception, since it relies on a conversation during development.

After [[humanizing-work-splitting]], the cutting half of the wiki has a procedure, and the position holds. The splitting patterns are triggered by features of the work and judged by whoever reviews it, so changing the executor leaves them standing. The source also supplies the one move a size-driven view would miss: a task that fails on value is combined, not cut further ([[component-task-masquerading]]).

[[ambig-swe]] sharpens the position with the wiki's only measurement. A task the literature could leave thin, because someone would ask, cannot be left thin when the executor tends not to ask: in the benchmark the six models rarely asked unprompted, and having the information in the task beat obtaining it during the run for all six. So the thesis for task content is that the conversation moves into the task before the agent starts ([[conversation-moves-into-the-task]]). This holds on stripped issues with a simulated user, and whether it holds with real requesters is open.

[[radzikowska-waiting-blocked]] extends the split between work and people to the board. Its criteria for waiting and blocked describe the work and survive a change of executor ([[waiting-versus-blocked]]). Its mechanisms for limits, tension and stress, describe people and do not, although the limit still stops an agent mechanically ([[blocked-work-counts-against-wip]]). It also gives the charter's first working test something it lacked, an observable failure: a task that reaches a stopped state after starting ([[sequence-versus-wait]]).

[[kanban-guide]] gives the board half of the wiki a definition to stand on, and it is mostly about the work: states, counts, timestamps. It removes one assumption and adds one question. Its WIP is set by position and not by activity, so stopped work counts unless a project declares otherwise ([[wip-control]]). The question is what a bound protects when the executor can run many tasks at once. This wiki's working answer is the attention of the people steering, which no source yet tests.

[[scrum-guide-2020]] mostly fails the charter, as expected, since it describes people and meetings. What survives is narrow and changes what the plugin must report. The commitment is a goal and the items are a forecast ([[commitment-over-a-period]]), so a period judged by items finished is judged on the wrong thing. The two guides turn out not to disagree on limits. The Kanban Guide bounds the moment and the Scrum Guide bounds the period, and each allows the other's addition. An agent changes that from a matter of taste to a gap: an iteration setup relied on people's hours to restrain the moment, and an agent has no such restraint ([[board-rules-by-setup]]). What this wiki keeps from Scrum is not Scrum by the Guide's own definition, so no benefit Scrum claims can be inherited by assumption.

[[wester-board-design]] gives the board half its first test of drawing: a column is for what happens in a predictable place, after which work goes forward ([[column-versus-attribute]]). It disagrees with the Kanban Maturity Model on holding areas, and the disagreement is resolved on [[parking-lot]] by narrowing the parking lot to a declared exception. It also corrects this wiki. A planned review is a handoff and not a block, so the test of finishing without waiting applies to the executor's part ([[sequence-versus-wait]]). This matters because an agent-run task almost always ends in a review. Wester's post, the Kanban Guide and the Kanban Playbook come from one school, so their agreement is not independent evidence.

[[kanban-playbook-workflow-states]] settles the charter's open question about states, as far as these sources can. There is no canonical column set. There are criteria for judging columns ([[judging-board-columns]]) and a set of distinctions any board must let a reader make ([[distinguishable-work-states]]). The Playbook treats the familiar five-column list as the failure case, and its remedy is to derive states from how work moved ([[mapping-workflow-states]]). The plugin therefore needs a classification to map columns onto and not a board to impose.

[[kanban-playbook-right-sizing]] gives a flow setup a size test: is there a good chance this finishes inside the SLE ([[size-ceiling]])? The page overstates it in two ways, since the question is itself an estimate, and since it reads an SLE it says is not reliable yet. What it contributes more durably is the point that the metrics count items, so the cut can flatter them both ways ([[hidden-wip-in-large-items]], [[component-task-masquerading]]). So the numbers alone cannot audit a cut, and the task text has to be read as well ([[compound-task-text-signs]]).

[[black-swan-qualitative-cod]] fills the empty centre of the ordering group, on a thin base. It is one unsigned practitioner article with no measurement, it calls itself a starting point, and its matrix image is lost, so only two of the nine combinations are the source's and the rest are this wiki's ([[ordering-by-value-and-urgency]]). What it contributes is a change of question: not what a task is worth, but what waiting loses ([[cost-of-delay]]). The ingredients are the requester's facts, which the repository cannot show, so the thesis that the conversation moves into the task extends to order. Before a task is ranked, someone has to say what it is worth and what waiting does to that, in a sentence that a later fact can contradict. Without that sentence, the rule fails the charter's test for a rule that chooses. Dividing by duration carries over less well, because it needs numbers the rating does not have and a constraint that an agent moves ([[cost-of-delay-over-duration]]).

[[zimmermann-good-bug-report]] is the base's second measurement, and it is about humans. It reinforces the split between the work and the people, and shows the split in a new place. Most of its ranking of report items describes experienced developers who know their system, and one item, expected behaviour, turns over when the reader is an agent ([[incoming-report-content]]). Its central result is that reporters know what is wanted and still do not supply it, which limits what any template can do. It also shows that a closed duplicate is often lost information ([[duplicate-reports]]), and it gives the board half a warning it lacked: a task's lifetime does not read the quality of its text ([[flow-metrics]]).

[[kubernetes-issue-triage]] removes the charter's prediction that intake triage would be self-authored, on a condition. It is a process written for one of the largest open-source projects, and most of it is people, permissions and tools. What survives is the shape: a marker for what nobody has read, a small set of outcomes, reproduction before acceptance, and triggers for what has stopped ([[triage-procedure]]). Its numbers do not survive, since 20 and 90 days encode a project of strangers ([[closing-what-no-longer-applies]]). So the thesis holds in a new place: the structure of a practice can transfer when its settings do not. It also gives order a second voice on one point, a top level that stops other work ([[interrupting-started-work]]), and a disagreement, one scale against two ratings, that resolves by whether a project ships in releases ([[urgent-versus-important]]).

[[montgomery-tracker-smells]] tests the charter's claim that a rule's sign lets the same page advise and audit, and it qualifies the claim. Practitioners shown 31 smells often answered "no" or "it depends". A sign shows that something may be wrong, and the project's workflow decides whether it is, so an audit reports a question ([[smell-is-not-a-finding]]). This is the first source to push against a design principle of this wiki and not only to fill a topic. It supports the rule on task content from another side: the one smell nobody refuted is a fact known and not in the task ([[fact-stranded-outside-the-task]]), which is the thesis about conversation seen on a board. It also corrects a sign this wiki would have used, since changing acceptance criteria was refuted by all who answered ([[testable-acceptance]]). The limits are firm. There are no thresholds, it reports perceptions, two of 26 participants work at small companies, and no agent appears. Its strongest item, the ignored issue ([[zombie-issue]]), rests on mechanisms of which one part transfers.

Across the board half one reading now recurs, and it is this wiki's and not any source's: with an agent executing, the scarce thing is the attention of the people steering. It underlies [[wip-control]], [[filling-a-period]], [[service-level-expectation]], [[active-versus-queued]], [[hidden-wip-in-large-items]], [[cost-of-delay-over-duration]] and [[interrupting-started-work]]. It is the largest untested claim in this wiki, and most of the agent notes on those pages rest on it.

## Where the position is weak

- Most signs on the task-content pages are this wiki's and not the sources', because the sources describe good tasks and rarely describe how a bad one looks. The same is true of the board pages: the guides state requirements and rarely say how a violation looks.
- Only [[ambig-swe]] and [[zimmermann-good-bug-report]] measure anything, the first on agents and stripped issues, the second by surveying people about bug reports. [[montgomery-tracker-smells]] is empirical too, but it reports interviews. Everything else here is practice from human teams, kept because its mechanism can be checked and not because its effect was shown. The two guides are definitions, and they offer no evidence that either works.
- Nothing yet compares a flow setup with an iteration setup. The selection rule on [[board-rules-by-setup]] is this wiki's, and the third setup, combining them, is permitted by both guides and tested by neither here.
- The Kanban sources other than [[radzikowska-waiting-blocked]] come from one school, so the column criteria on [[judging-board-columns]] have one voice behind them. [[montgomery-tracker-smells]] adds a second, on three criteria only, as perceptions.
- The failure signs have no thresholds, and this wiki has not supplied any. So an audit from this base can say that something is there and cannot say that there is too much of it. No sign has been tried on a real board ([[smell-is-not-a-finding]]).
- The ordering pages rest on one article with no measurement, and seven of the nine ranks on [[ordering-by-value-and-urgency]] are this wiki's derivation. Reinertsen's book, the obvious second voice, reached the base only as a third-party summary and was rejected.
- Nothing yet covers an agent's own estimate, or what replaces a team's experience in sizing ([[size-ceiling]]).
- No page yet says how to give a date under a flow setup ([[commitment-over-a-period]]).
