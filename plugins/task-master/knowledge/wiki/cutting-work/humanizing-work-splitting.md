# The Humanizing Work Guide to Splitting User Stories

This is a practitioner guide from the Humanizing Work company. The raw file's `authors` field is empty; the text identifies the writers only as people who have been "practicing, writing about, and coaching others on user story splitting since at least 2005" (L37) and names "Richard" as the creator of the flowchart (L37, L161). The page also sells the firm's course and workshop (L39, L41, L420-L427).

## Thesis

The guide argues that most teams cut along the wrong axis:

> Instead of ending up with small vertical slices through their architecture, they get stories that look more like tasks or architectural components and fail to experience the value or feedback small stories should provide.
>
> — [[humanizing-work-splitting]], L45

The authors' remedy is a three-step flowchart: make the input a real increment of value, apply patterns until it is small enough, and then evaluate which split to keep.

## What this wiki dropped, and why

- **The practice-session programme** (L350-L372), including "only about 2.5-3 hours of practice" (L350). It scripts a meeting and develops people's skills, which the charter leaves out, and the figure is a claim of effect with no setting.
- **Vertical slices and scale** (L374-L416): feature teams, component teams, 100+ people on a product, and the claim that coordination load "increases exponentially" (L404). These answer how to organize many teams.
- **The list of benefits of working in vertical slices** (L147-L153). It asserts outcomes without an observable for any of them.
- **"Working in vertical slices was the key habit"** (L155). The mapping behind this is not shown, so it cannot be checked.
- **Download counts** (L37) and the claim about how quickly splitting can be learned (L47). The first is a credential and the second concerns people's skills.
- **The comparison with Jobs to be Done** (L55). It locates stories in the solution space, which changes nothing the plugin outputs.

## How to read it

Read it for the mechanisms: its triggers and its two tests name things that can be seen in a request or a backlog, so it passes the charter's practitioner test. It offers no data. The evidence is the authors' coaching of human Scrum teams, stated in terms of sprints, a Product Owner and planning meetings, so each page drawn from it carries a note on whether the claim survives an agent executing and a person steering.
