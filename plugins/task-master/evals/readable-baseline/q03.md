Points alone cannot tell you. Rising points per iteration is a sign, not a finding. Separate the two by comparing the points with something the estimates did not touch: the count and the observed size of the items that finished. The base states the sign this way: points per iteration rising "while the count and observed size of finished items stay flat" means the estimates moved and the work did not (`wiki/reading-history/velocity.md`, "What it measures and does not"). This wiki says velocity moves with the estimates as well as the work, because it is a sum in the team's own arbitrary scale.

**What to check, in order**

1. **Count finished items per period.** Take the tasks that reached a finished state in each iteration over the three months and count them, ignoring points. If the count is flat or falling while points rise, the scale is drifting. If the count falls and the points hold, the work may be getting harder or the cut may be changing. (`wiki/reading-history/velocity.md`; the count is the Throughput measure it points to for projects that do not estimate.)
2. **Compare like with like.** Find tasks of the same kind that closed early in the window and late in it, and compare their points and their time on the board. Same kind of task, bigger number, same time lived: the scale moved. Same points, longer time lived: the work or the rate moved. Time lived is read from board history, so nobody has to log hours (`wiki/sizing/comparing-estimate-to-actual.md`, "The test").
3. **Look for estimates changed after the fact.** The signs on `wiki/sizing/re-estimate-when-relative-size-changes.md` (section "Hindsight Re-Estimation"): a task's estimate edited after it closed while open tasks of its kind kept theirs, velocity rising while the count of closed tasks does not, and a stated reason of "it took longer" rather than "it is bigger than something named". That page explains why the pattern is dangerous: it mixes scales in one calculation and improves the forecast without more being delivered. Its stated trigger is disappointment with the number, and it adds that the same pressure can reach a person steering an agent.
4. **Check whether anyone reads points as a target.** `wiki/sizing/comparing-estimate-to-actual.md` cites Cohn on evaluation pressure ("evaluation apprehension") and, as its own test of the choice having gone wrong, size steps drifting upward after a comparison starts while time lived does not. Where points are reported upward or compared between people, drift is the expected result.
5. **Check whether the baseline is still shared.** If different people or sessions estimate, a reference set of finished tasks keeps a size meaning the same thing (`wiki/sizing/estimation-baseline.md`). Signs there: comparable tasks carrying very different sizes depending on who estimated them, or actual effort per point differing across estimators. The page marks the second as ambiguous, since it can also be a real difference in the work. Its note on agent sessions (separate sessions share no memory of past sizing, so they can behave like separate estimators) is this wiki's own transfer and untested.

**What each outcome means**

- Points up, count flat, time lived per task flat: inflation. Do not rewrite old sizes. Freeze a reference set of closed tasks, estimate new work against it, and recompute the forecast from the measured rate (`wiki/sizing/velocity-self-correction.md`). That page says it "does not matter if our estimates are correct" so long as "they are consistent", and drift is a loss of consistency.
- Time lived per task up, count down, points flat: the work or the process got slower or harder. Look at where the time sits before blaming difficulty (waiting and blocked time, review queues). The velocity page notes that with an agent executing, the limit on the rate may sit in how fast results can be reviewed, which is a change in the steering and not in the work.
- Both moving: some of each. Item 2 on a handful of matched tasks apportions it.

**Two things this base does not settle**

- Harder work shows up legitimately as a class of tasks re-estimated together, and the base's rule is to change estimates only when relative size changed for a subset you can name (`wiki/sizing/re-estimate-when-relative-size-changes.md`). If a nameable group of tasks (a new area of the system, a new kind of integration) took over the backlog in these three months, rising points may be honest. Check what the backlog is made of now against three months ago. The base gives no threshold for how much change counts.
- Outside the base, general practice: check that the iteration lengths, holidays, team size, and the model or tools an agent runs on did not change over the window, since each moves the rate without touching either difficulty or the scale. The base only says that history from before a change in how an agent runs may not forecast what follows (`wiki/sizing/velocity-self-correction.md`, agent executor note).

**Setup assumption.** This assumes iterations with estimates kept. If the project has no iterations, sum over a fixed span of calendar time instead. If it has no estimates at all, the question does not arise, and the count of finished items is the measure. Which of these is yours?
