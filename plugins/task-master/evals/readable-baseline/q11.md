The chart is the cumulative flow diagram (CFD). It plots cumulative arrivals and cumulative departures over time, and optionally the same for each step between them. Work is piling up when the arrival line (top) is steeper than the departure line (bottom), so the gap between them, which is the WIP, keeps widening. Read the vertical distance between two lines for WIP, and look for a band that grows or stays thick for where it sits (`wiki/showing-work/cumulative-flow-diagram.md`, `wiki/showing-work/reading-a-cfd.md`).

**How to read it for piling up**

- Compare the slopes of the top and bottom lines. A top line that is steeper than the bottom line means work enters faster than it leaves. WIP then grows, and Vacanti says that "will almost certainly lead to an increase in Cycle Time" (`reading-a-cfd.md`). Roughly parallel lines mean arrivals equal departures on average.
- To see where the work sits, draw a band per board state, with queue ("done") states separate from active ones. A band that widens, or a queue band that stays thick, marks the step to look at. Two bands (arrivals and departures only) answer "are we taking in more than we finish" but not where. A chart cannot show a state the board does not record (`cumulative-flow-diagram.md`).
- The chart must be built from each item's date of entry into each step, not from column counts. A line that falls is the sign of a count-built or malformed chart, and any reading from it is void (`cumulative-flow-diagram.md`).
- A short mismatch is not the pattern. The assumption concerns rates "on average", and the source gives no window length. This wiki reads the sign as a slope difference that persists with the gap still widening (`reading-a-cfd.md`).

**What the CFD cannot tell you**

1. **Why, or where the bottleneck is.** A wide band says where items wait, not why. The base's rule is that a finding from the shape alone raises a question about variability and does not name a bottleneck. Vacanti: "It is impossible to spot a systemic bottleneck." The Siemens case in the same book credits the CFD with showing where capacity was short, and the base keeps that as a counter-voice, not the rule (`reading-a-cfd.md`, `cumulative-flow-diagram.md`).
2. **Whether the process is healthy.** A balanced chart is necessary and not sufficient: "Pretty CFD pictures could still mask underlying process problems." A report that cites balance with no look at item ages or abandoned work is the sign of misreading (`cumulative-flow-diagram.md`).
3. **What will happen.** It records only the past, and a chart with a projection plotted is not a CFD. Forecasts go elsewhere, to Monte Carlo on throughput (`cumulative-flow-diagram.md`, `wiki/showing-work/which-chart-for-which-plan-question.md`).
4. **Which items are old, or how long open work has been open.** The cycle-time reading is valid only for finished items, and the chart shows bands and averages, not single items. Open-work age needs item-level data (`cumulative-flow-diagram.md`, `which-chart-for-which-plan-question.md`).
5. **Exact cycle time.** The horizontal distance gives an approximate average only, and the items at the two ends of the line are not necessarily the same items. Report it labelled as approximate (`cumulative-flow-diagram.md`).
6. **What left, or how.** A departure means an item crossed the finish boundary. It does not say whether the result was delivered or abandoned, so abandoned work raises the bottom line too (`cumulative-flow-diagram.md`).
7. **Assumption violations, without context.** Many broken assumptions of Little's Law do not show on the chart, so a clean chart is not a clean process. Without the project's context a reading yields questions and no conclusions (`cumulative-flow-diagram.md`, `reading-a-cfd.md`).

**Setup and scale**

The CFD is arithmetic on state changes, so it works under any setup (`cumulative-flow-diagram.md`). In an iteration setup, the iteration burndown answers "will we finish this iteration's work", not "is work piling up" (`which-chart-for-which-plan-question.md`). The base says only that Vacanti's example is far larger than a board kept by a few people, and that whether a CFD says much on a board moving a few items a week is untested here. Reading these pages together, a short stretch on a sparse board deserves little weight.

Where an agent executes and a person accepts, the answer depends on what the bottom line means: "agent finished" or "person accepted". A gap measured to acceptance may be a gap in the person's review, not in execution (`reading-a-cfd.md`).
