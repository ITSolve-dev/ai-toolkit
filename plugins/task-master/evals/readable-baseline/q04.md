Do not accept "cannot be split" as a finding, and do not let the task start as it stands. Treat it as a claim to test: ask the author for the list of what is inside, and run the cuts that apply. If none applies, the task stays whole only as a recorded, deliberate exception that a person steering has accepted.

**Procedure**

1. **Ask what the claim means.** In this base, "unsplittable" most often means the task is a layer or an activity, not a unit of value: "When people bring us their “unsplittable” stories, they turn out to be tasks or components masquerading as stories." (`wiki/cutting-work/component-task-masquerading.md`). Check the task text for a layer, a component or a document as the wanted thing. If so, do not cut smaller. Combine it with its neighbours until together they are an increment of value, then cut that across the layers (same page, and `wiki/cutting-work/vertical-slice.md`).
2. **Read the text for hidden parts** (`wiki/cutting-work/compound-task-text-signs.md`): conjunctions, generic terms ("experience"), plurals, acceptance criteria that each stand alone. Ask for the list behind any broad title. A sign is a reason to look, not proof.
3. **Run the nine patterns** in `wiki/cutting-work/story-splitting-patterns.md` (workflow steps, operations, rule variations, data variations, entry methods, major effort, simple/complex, defer performance, spike). A cut by process step, one task per step, is the wrong way to do pattern 1. Where several cuts work, take the one that lets a low-value piece be dropped, then the one giving more equal pieces (`wiki/cutting-work/choosing-between-splits.md`). For a first slice of something new, cut through the part most likely to surprise.
4. **If the obstacle is not knowing how it would be built**, a time-boxed spike is the last resort, with questions as acceptance criteria, and the remainder stays as a placeholder re-estimated afterwards (`wiki/cutting-work/spike-as-last-resort.md`). Vacanti's version of the same options: "break it up, or change the fidelity, or spike it to get more information" (`wiki/sizing/right-sizing.md`).
5. **If it is not close to starting, it may stay large for now.** Cohn: large items are placeholders, and "If you need to create a sixteen-hour task during iteration planning, do so. However, once the task is more adequately understood, augment or replace it." (`wiki/sizing/size-ceiling.md`). The deadline stays firm: nothing above the ceiling starts. Whether it is cut now depends on distance from starting, or on whether a better estimate would change a decision (same page).
6. **If it is at the top of the order and truly resists every cut**, taking it as is stays open to whoever steers. Record that the size check said no, so a later overrun reads as an accepted risk and not a surprise (`wiki/sizing/right-sizing.md`, a resolution marked there as this wiki's own). Reading these pages together (my inference), this is the point to also say what the cost is: one task is one result reviewed at once, and the hidden work reappears at review (`wiki/sizing/hidden-wip-in-large-items.md`, agent executor note). Then watch it against the 85th percentile or the SLE and recut when it passes (`wiki/sizing/overrun-triggers-a-recut.md`).

**What breaks the other way.** Starting an oversized task unchanged is the failure these pages describe: a count of one that holds several tasks' worth of work, showing up later as a cycle time outlier. Cutting it by layer to satisfy the ceiling is the opposite failure: pieces that close fast and deliver nothing a user can see.

**Which numbers apply depends on the setup.** The ceiling is the project's SLE question under flow (can it finish inside the SLE), or about a sixth of iteration capacity under iterations without an SLE; the outer limit is one period (`wiki/sizing/size-ceiling.md`). I do not know which applies here. Which is it: iterations or continuous flow, and does the project keep an SLE?
