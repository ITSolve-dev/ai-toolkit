# Synthesis

This is this wiki's own reading of what its pages add up to. It rests on seven books and two articles so far, [[pragmatic-programmer]], [[elegant-objects]], [[code-complete]], [[object-oriented-thought-process]], [[automata-based-programming]], [[larman-applying-uml-and-patterns]], [[fowler-refactoring]], [[railway-oriented-programming]] and [[against-railway-oriented-programming]], so it records their arguments as seen through this charter and is not a settled view. Further sources should strengthen or challenge it.

## The thesis

Good code is code whose next change is cheap, and whose author knows why it works. Almost every rule here serves one of those two ends.

**Cheap change.** *The Pragmatic Programmer* puts one value under its design principles: [[easier-to-change]]. The rules divide by which cost they attack. [[dry]] attacks a fact held in several places, so one change needs several edits. [[orthogonality]] and [[coupling]] attack a change reaching parts that had no stake in it. [[reversibility]] and [[replaceable-code-over-forecast]] attack the cost of a wrong guess, and prefer a seam to a forecast. [[software-entropy]] explains why these costs compound: visible neglect invites more of it.

*Elegant Objects* names a near neighbour of that value, maintainability measured as the time needed to understand code (p. 17 of [[elegant-objects]]). This wiki reads the two as one end seen from two sides: code a reader understands quickly is code a reader can change cheaply.

*Code Complete* gives the same end its most explicit form. Its primary technical imperative is [[managing-complexity]]: a design fails if working on one part does not let the reader safely ignore most of the others (p. 77 of [[code-complete]]). Its closing chapter treats readability as the reader's side of that test ([[code-for-the-reader]]) and conventions as the means of removing arbitrary differences ([[coding-conventions]]). This wiki reads the three books' values as one: cheap change (*The Pragmatic Programmer*), time to understand (*Elegant Objects*), and how much a reader must hold at once (*Code Complete*). The third is the one with countable signs, and many pages here use its thresholds as cues to read ([[design-warning-signs]], [[decision-point-count]], [[tame-deep-nesting]]).

**Knowing why.** The second thread runs through the wiki under different names. [[programming-by-coincidence]] names it for behaviour, [[representation-that-fits-by-coincidence]] for data, [[remove-calls-you-cannot-justify]] for leftover calls, and [[prove-dont-assume]] and [[select-isnt-broken]] for diagnosis. The remedy each time is to turn a belief into something executed: a contract ([[design-by-contract]]), an assertion ([[assert-the-impossible]]) or a test that has been seen to fail ([[sabotage-your-tests]]).

The two ends meet in feedback. [[small-steps-with-feedback]], [[tracer-code]] and [[refactor-in-small-tested-steps]] keep each change small enough to judge, and [[find-bugs-once]] makes each lesson permanent.

## Changing code safely

Fowler's *Refactoring* fills the gap the earlier synthesis named: a book devoted to changing code without changing what it does. Its thesis turns the first thread into a procedure. A change is cheap when it is a sequence of steps each small enough that the tests judge it, and a red test means stepping back to the last green state ([[refactor-in-small-tested-steps]]); restructuring and adding behaviour are kept apart ([[do-not-refactor-while-adding-features]]), and restructuring is done just before the work that needs it ([[preparatory-refactoring]]). A change that seems to need every caller edited at once is broken up by expanding first and migrating callers one at a time ([[change-function-declaration]], [[parallel-change]], [[extract-before-changing-many-callers]]). None of this works without tests that run on every change ([[self-testing-code]]).

This wiki reads the catalogue's main contribution to the base as its inverse pairs. Most refactorings come with their opposite, and the book's worth for an agent is the condition between them: extract or inline ([[extract-function]], [[inline-function]]), pass a value or let the function find it ([[parameter-or-query]]), function or command object ([[function-or-command-object]]), pull up or push down ([[pull-up-method]], [[push-down-method-and-field]]), subclasses or a field ([[replace-type-code-with-subclasses]], [[subclass-that-differs-only-in-data]]). That matches the charter's view that a page earns its place by the judgement it supplies, not by naming the rule.

On inheritance Fowler takes a fourth position beside the three object books: use it first, and replace it by delegation when it rubs, namely on a second axis of variation, a run-time change of kind, or an instance extending its kind ([[replace-subclass-with-delegate]], [[replace-superclass-with-delegate]]). This wiki reads that as compatible with *The Pragmatic Programmer*'s distrust of inheritance once both are stated per setting: inside one module with one axis, inheritance stands; where one of those signs shows, both books call for delegation.

## Where the shape of code comes from

The two books pull in different directions here, and that is the wiki's largest open tension.

*The Pragmatic Programmer* shifts weight from objects holding state towards data moving through stages. [[programs-transform-data]] and [[pass-state-dont-hoard-it]] make that case, [[inheritance-tax]] prices the older habit, and [[shared-state-is-incorrect-state]] gives the sharpest reason, since shared mutable state is what concurrency cannot survive.

*Elegant Objects* keeps the object and changes what it is. An object stands for the data it encapsulates and is not a container of procedures over it ([[object-as-representative]]). From that come its rules on names ([[name-classes-for-what-they-are]]), construction ([[one-primary-constructor]], [[constructors-without-code]], [[new-only-in-secondary-constructors]]), surface ([[no-getters-and-setters]], [[public-methods-implement-interfaces]], [[fewer-than-five-public-methods]]) and the bans on statics and singletons ([[no-static-methods]], [[no-singletons]]).

This wiki reads the two as converging more than they appear to. Both books reject mutable shared state: the first by passing values through stages, the second by making objects immutable ([[immutable-objects]], [[prefer-constant-objects]]). Both reject inheritance for reuse: the first for its coupling ([[inheritance-tax]]), the second for its overrides ([[final-or-abstract]]). Both prefer composition: a pipeline of functions in the first, nested decorators in the second ([[composable-decorators]], [[declarative-style]]). A lazy decorator chain is close to a pipeline evaluated on demand. Where they truly differ is where behaviour lives, and this wiki does not choose one paradigm: each page carries a `paradigm` field so a rule for objects is not applied to functional code.

## Where the shape of code comes from, the third view

*Code Complete* sits between the two. It keeps classes as abstract data types with a coherent interface ([[abstract-data-type]], [[class-interface-coherent-abstraction]]), as *Elegant Objects* does, but tolerates getters, enumerations and some global data behind access routines, which *Elegant Objects* forbids. It shares *The Pragmatic Programmer*'s distrust of inheritance for reuse ([[containment-or-inheritance]], [[deep-inheritance-hierarchy]]). Most of its pages work below the level where the paradigms disagree: routines, variables, conditions and loops, where its advice holds for any `paradigm`. Where it does disagree with the others, the owning page resolves it per setting; the list is on [[code-complete]].

## The introductory view of objects

Weisfeld's *The Object-Oriented Thought Process* adds little the other three do not hold, and that is its value here: it is the conventional teaching of objects that the other books build on or push against. Its centre is [[interface-and-implementation]], with the minimal interface ([[minimize-member-accessibility]]), private attributes ([[uniform-access]]) and a constructor that leaves a stable state ([[initialize-every-member-in-every-constructor]]). On composition it supplies what the others leave implicit: the split between parts and collaborators ([[aggregation-vs-association]]), the count of an association carried into field types ([[association-cardinality-in-field-types]]) and the reason composition pays, Simon's near-decomposability ([[near-decomposability]]). This wiki reads it as standing with *Code Complete* on accessors and with *Elegant Objects* on narrow interfaces, and against *Elegant Objects* on Singleton, which it teaches as the model creational pattern; [[no-singletons]] resolves that. Its later chapters on XML, persistence and web clients yield three claims about data crossing a boundary: validate against a declared structure ([[validate-exchanged-data-against-a-declared-schema]]), check on the side that owns the data ([[barricade]]), and treat a stored object as state without its invariants ([[object-persistence]]).

## Where a responsibility goes

Larman's GRASP and GoF chapters supply what the other object books assume: a method for deciding which class gets a piece of behaviour ([[responsibility-driven-design]]). The default is the class holding the information ([[information-expert]]), with [[creator]] and [[controller]] for the two cases Expert does not settle, and the choice between candidates made by [[low-coupling]] and [[cohesion]]. When the expert would gain a dependency on something unstable or lose its focus, a made-up class takes the work ([[pure-fabrication]]). This wiki reads that as the method behind several rules already here: [[tell-dont-ask]] is Expert seen from the caller, and [[god-class]] is what follows when a controller does the work instead of delegating it.

His second claim is that the GoF patterns are these principles applied to recurring variation ([[patterns-as-specializations-of-grasp]], [[protected-variations]]). That gives the pattern pages one shared test, whether the variation a pattern protects against is present in the code, and it is the same test [[do-not-force-a-design-pattern]] applies from the other side; Larman's own name for its failure is pattern-itis. This wiki reads him as standing with *Code Complete* on objects as owners of their data, with *Elegant Objects* on polymorphism over type switches, and against *Elegant Objects* on Singleton, which [[no-singletons]] resolves per setting. His controllers, factories and adapters are also classes named for what they do, which [[name-classes-for-what-they-are]] rejects; no page yet states the condition between the two, and the open questions below record it.

## Behaviour that depends on history

Polikarpova and Shalyto's *Automata-Based Programming* treats one problem the other books touch only in passing: an entity whose reaction to the same input depends on what came before ([[complex-behavior]]). *The Pragmatic Programmer* offers a state machine as a table ([[finite-state-machine]]); this book makes the state machine the organising idea of the code. Its answer has three parts. The state that selects actions is named and kept in one place, separate from the data that only stores results ([[control-and-computational-states]], [[replace-flags-with-explicit-states]]). The code mirrors the transition graph so it can be checked as a graph ([[state-machine-as-switch-on-state]], [[transition-conditions-consistent-and-complete]]). Decisions live in the automaton and data in a controlled object reached through named queries and commands ([[automated-object]], [[separate-control-object-from-state-logic]]).

This wiki reads the book as joining the object books more than it first appears. Its object-oriented chapter wraps the automaton in a class whose public methods are the events and whose state is private ([[automated-class]]), which is encapsulation as *Code Complete* and *Elegant Objects* ask for it; and it reads complex behaviour as a form of polymorphism, one subtype per control state, which leads to the State pattern ([[contract-of-state-dependent-method]], [[switch-on-state-vs-state-pattern]]). Where it parts from them is the switch: the object books push a switch over a type code towards polymorphism, and this book keeps a switch over a control state as its main template. The line between the two shapes is whether the object's own methods reassign the value switched on.

## Failure

On failure the books agree more than they differ. The first two want a failure to be loud and near its cause: [[crash-early]] in the first, fail-fast and [[recover-only-at-entry-points]] in the second. *Elegant Objects* adds what the first leaves open: no null in or out ([[never-return-null]], [[no-null-arguments]]), exceptions that keep their cause ([[chain-exceptions]]), and resources bound to an object's lifetime ([[exception-safe-release]]). *Code Complete* adds the frame both leave implicit: defensive code is a boundary ([[barricade]]) and a chosen reaction on a spectrum from robustness to correctness ([[robustness-versus-correctness]]), decided once for the system and applied consistently ([[consistent-error-handling-strategy]]). The pages divide these shapes by one question, whether a correct program can meet the failure, which [[assertion-or-error-handling]] decides.

Wlaschin's two articles add a second question for code that carries failures as values: whether the caller is meant to handle this failure as part of its normal work. The 2013 article supplies the plumbing for a result type ([[handle-errors-in-stages-or-in-the-pipeline]]); its author's 2019 critique keeps out of the result whatever only the top of the program deals with, which then travels as an exception to an entry point ([[wrap-values-between-transformations]], [[recover-only-at-entry-points]]). This wiki reads the critique as bringing a result-type convention under the same fail-fast stance the books take.

## Tensions the wiki resolves

Taken alone, several rules contradict each other. This wiki's value lies mostly in the condition that separates them.

## How far this can be trusted

The books state rules and reasons, and rarely state a sign in code or a limit. Most of what an agent applies on a rule page is therefore this wiki's own extension, marked as such on each page. That marking matters more than any single page. The answers in *The Pragmatic Programmer*'s appendix need the same care, since one of them fails its own invariants, as [[design-by-contract]] shows.

*The Pragmatic Programmer* and *Elegant Objects* argue from experience and not from measurement. *Elegant Objects* is deliberately radical, its author expects to be wrong in places (p. 13), and its numbers are by his admission taken from his head. Its quotations come from noisy OCR and have not been checked against the page images. Where they agree, that is two practitioners' views, not independent confirmation. *Code Complete* cites studies for many claims (defect rates, inspection yields, maintenance shares), which makes it the most checkable of the three, but most of its figures are from the 1970s to the 1990s, and several of its quotations were transcribed from extracted text and await checking against the page image. *The Object-Oriented Thought Process* has a clean text layer, so its quotations were checked against `raw/`, but it is an introduction: its listings sometimes contradict its prose, and a few of its statements are slips the pages record rather than carry ([[object-oriented-thought-process]]). *Automata-Based Programming* also has a clean text layer, but it is the method's own advocate, written by the originator of the technology, and argues from worked examples; its listings contain typos the pages do not reproduce ([[automata-based-programming]]). *Applying UML and Patterns* was read in English from an e-book with a clean text layer, so its quotations are checked against `raw/`, but the e-book has no printed page numbers and its pages cite section and PDF page; many of its designs are UML figures `raw/` does not hold, so details taken from them are marked as inferred ([[larman-applying-uml-and-patterns]]). *Refactoring* was also read in English from an e-book with a clean text layer and no printed page numbers, so its pages cite chapter, refactoring name and PDF page, and its quotations were checked against `raw/`. It argues from worked examples and a practitioner's experience, not from measurement, and its catalogue gives motivations and mechanics rather than signs or limits, so on its rule pages most signs and limits are this wiki's own ([[fowler-refactoring]]).

## Open questions

- Do signs written for existing code help when writing new code? The charter predicts they help review and repair more, and the pilot records this per mode.
- Does *Code Complete*'s weight change the base's balance? It is now the source of most pages, so its view (classes as abstract data types, tolerated getters and enumerations) could dominate reviews where the other two books disagree. Review should show whether the per-setting resolutions hold.
- Do its numeric thresholds (seven members, 10 decision points, three levels of nesting) produce findings or only cues? [[design-warning-signs]] treats them as cues; pilot use should confirm agents do not report a count as a finding.
- Does the second source change the pilot's result on object design? The showcase found all variants weak there, and *Elegant Objects* was ingested to fill that gap; the repeat evaluation decides.
- Which applies where, transformation or representative objects? The wiki keeps both and separates them by `paradigm`, but has no test for choosing a paradigm for new code. A source on functional programming is still missing.
- Will agents apply the refactoring pages as recipes or as judgement? *Refactoring* filled the gap on changing code safely, but many of its pages are `method` pages with mechanics, and an agent may run mechanics where the inverse refactoring or no change was the answer. Review should show whether the inverse pairs and their conditions are being read.
- Does Fowler's inheritance-first stance shift reviews of hierarchies? It is resolved per setting against *The Pragmatic Programmer* and *Elegant Objects*, but the pages that now own hierarchy shapes (pull up, push down, collapse, the delegate pages) are mostly his; review should show whether they draw findings on hierarchies the other books would leave alone or reject.
- Will agents over-apply the state-machine pages? *Automata-Based Programming* advises its method only for entities with complex behaviour, and [[complex-behavior]] carries the test; review should show whether code with two trivial modes draws findings from the switch and table pages.
- How often will the project convention override *Elegant Objects*? Many of its pages name frameworks, mocking tools and language idioms that win. If review finds the override is the common case, those pages may cost more findings than they repair.
- Do Larman's case-study designs carry over outside a layered application? His controllers and fabrications sit in a UI, domain and services layering the charter takes as given; review should show whether [[controller]] draws findings in code with no such layers.
- When is a class named for what it does (a controller, a factory, an adapter, as Larman designs them) acceptable against [[name-classes-for-what-they-are]]? The two pages do not yet name each other, so a review could report Larman's own designs as violations.
- Some pages admit the charter's strict sign only with difficulty: [[easier-to-change]] has no sign of its own, and [[software-entropy]] has a local sign for only one of its two mechanisms. Pilot use should show whether such pages earn their place as reasoning.
